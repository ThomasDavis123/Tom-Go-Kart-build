A build, start to finish

Building an electric go-kart

In Year 12, I built one from scratch: welded frame, wiring, paint, all of it. This is what that actually looked like.

Finished, wired, and running — a few months after this was just a stack of steel tube.

I'd never welded anything before this. I wanted to understand how a vehicle actually goes together — not from a textbook, but by cutting, welding, and wiring one myself. So over one build I taught myself chassis fabrication, basic electrical work, and enough patience to redo things that didn't sit right the first time.

Here's the build in three stages.

Frame

Cutting and welding the chassis

This was the part with the steepest learning curve. Every joint had to be cut, fitted, and welded square enough that the frame would actually sit true once the wheels went on — small errors here would have shown up everywhere downstream. I worked out the layout myself: track width, seat position, where the motor and battery would need to sit for weight to balance out.

Wiring

Motor, battery, and switchgear

This was the first time I'd wired anything load-bearing — getting the kill switch and power circuit right mattered more here than anywhere else in the build.

The electronics were new territory. I had to figure out how to mount the motor so the drive line actually lined up, run wiring that wouldn't chafe or work loose once the kart was moving, and get a switch panel in place that was simple enough to trust under load.

Finish

Bodywork, paint, and the final assembly

Once the mechanicals worked, I panelled and painted it — black deck, green frame and steering — and added an underglow strip mostly because I could. It's a small detail, but it's the part that made the kart feel finished rather than just functional.

What stuck with me most was how much the build was a loop of getting something wrong, figuring out why, and fixing it — not that different from how I'd describe good engineering generally. It's the main reason I want to keep doing this kind of hands-on, iterative work at a bigger scale with Monash Motorsport.

Tom — Mechanical Engineering, Monash University
